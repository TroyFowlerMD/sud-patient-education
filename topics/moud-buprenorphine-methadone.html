How Medications Work

Opioids work by binding to receptors in the brain and body. Different medications interact with those receptors in different ways — and that difference matters for safety and treatment.

💊 Buprenorphine (Suboxone, Subutex, Sublocade)

Buprenorphine is a partial agonist — it activates opioid receptors, but only partway. Think of it like a car engine that runs but only revs to a limited speed. This ceiling effect means that above a certain dose, the effect levels off, which provides some protection against overdose.

Buprenorphine also binds very tightly to opioid receptors — tighter than most other opioids. This strong binding can reduce the effect of other opioids taken on top of it and helps suppress cravings and withdrawal around the clock.

Most formulations include naloxone (Narcan) to discourage injection misuse. When taken as a film or tablet under the tongue as prescribed, the naloxone is not significantly absorbed and does not interfere with treatment.

Buprenorphine is usually prescribed in an office or clinic setting and can be taken at home after early stabilization — making it more flexible for outpatient life.

Starting buprenorphine Starting too soon after last opioid use can cause precipitated withdrawal — sudden, severe withdrawal symptoms. This is a known medical risk, not an unpredictable reaction. Your treatment team will time the start carefully.

💊 Methadone (Methadose, Dolophine)

Methadone is a full opioid agonist — it fully activates opioid receptors, similar to other opioids, but at a carefully controlled dose and in a monitored clinical setting. Because it fully activates receptors, it is highly effective at suppressing withdrawal and cravings, especially for people with high opioid tolerance.

Methadone also has a very long half-life — it stays in the body for 24 to 36 hours or longer after a single dose. This means it provides stable, around-the-clock suppression of withdrawal and cravings with once-daily dosing. But it also means that the medication accumulates over time during the first days of treatment.

⚠ Important safety note Because methadone builds up slowly, a dose that seems tolerable on day one can become too strong after several days if increases happen too quickly. This is why methadone for addiction is dispensed at licensed opioid treatment programs (OTPs), not regular pharmacies, and why dose increases require medical supervision and careful monitoring.

Methadone requires daily clinic attendance, especially early in treatment. This structure can be helpful for some patients but creates real barriers for others — including transportation, work schedules, and distance from an OTP.

Full agonists keep rising into overdose territory with increasing dose. Buprenorphine's effect levels off (ceiling effect), providing a margin of safety. Antagonists (naltrexone, naloxone) block receptors without activating them.

💊 Naltrexone (Vivitrol) — A Different Approach

Naltrexone is also FDA-approved for opioid use disorder. Unlike buprenorphine and methadone, it is an antagonist — it blocks opioid receptors rather than activating them. It has no opioid activity and will not cause dependence.

The challenge: starting naltrexone too soon after opioid use causes severe precipitated withdrawal. Patients typically need to be opioid-free for 7–10 days after short-acting opioids and 10–14 days after long-acting opioids before starting. Because this guide focuses on starting medication during or soon after detox, buprenorphine and methadone are usually the main options. Naltrexone is worth discussing with your provider if full abstinence before initiation is achievable.

Staying in Treatment Matters

A medication only protects you if you can stay on it. That is why the "best" medication is not just the one that sounds best on paper — it is the one that is effective, tolerable, and realistic to continue.

In one large randomized trial, more people completed 24 weeks of treatment with methadone than with buprenorphine: 74% with methadone compared with 46% with buprenorphine. In plain language, that means people assigned to methadone completed treatment about 1.6 times as often in that study.

Trial context matters. Access, patient preference, dose adequacy, and real-world factors all affect outcomes. Methadone completion reached 80% when maximum doses reached or exceeded 60 mg/day.

This does not mean methadone is automatically better for everyone. It means methadone's structure and dose range can help some people stay in treatment, while buprenorphine's easier outpatient access can help other people stay in treatment.

Dose Adequacy

Underdosing can be a major reason medication "doesn't work." If a person still has withdrawal, cravings, or ongoing opioid use, the dose may need reassessment.

For buprenorphine, many people do well at 16 mg per day, while some need 24 mg per day. For methadone, the effective dose range is broader, and many patients need doses above very low starting ranges to fully suppress withdrawal and cravings.

The goal of dosing The goal is not to feel intoxicated. The goal is to feel normal enough to sleep, eat, think clearly, participate in treatment, avoid withdrawal, manage cravings, and build recovery.

Overdose Protection

Buprenorphine and methadone reduce overdose risk partly by stabilizing opioid tolerance, reducing cravings, decreasing return to unpredictable street opioids, and helping people remain connected to care. Buprenorphine also has a ceiling effect and strong receptor binding, which can provide some protection against other opioids.

But neither medication makes overdose impossible. Risk increases with fentanyl exposure, missed medication, stopping treatment, taking extra methadone, using benzodiazepines or alcohol, or returning to opioid use after tolerance has dropped.

🔔 Everyone with opioid use disorder should have naloxone Family members, partners, roommates, and close supports should know where naloxone is kept and how to use it. Call 911 immediately in an overdose emergency.

How Long Should I Stay on Medication?

There is no one-size-fits-all answer. For many people with severe opioid use disorder, long-term medication treatment is the safest plan. A useful way to think about MOUD is like maintenance treatment for other chronic illnesses — people with diabetes may need insulin long-term, and people with opioid use disorder may need medication to keep the illness stable.

Some people can eventually taper off medication and maintain recovery. The harder question is when the risk is low enough to make stopping a good idea. In general, it is better not to rush.

A practical approach is to first build a stable recovery: consistent sobriety, stable housing, healthy routines, meaningful work or volunteering, supportive relationships, treatment engagement, and a plan for stress. After a long period of stability — often years, and at least more than a few months — it may be reasonable to discuss a slow, careful taper with an outpatient provider.

If Stopping Later

Stopping should be slow and planned. It should not be done suddenly, secretly, or during a major life crisis. Before tapering, ask: What has changed that makes relapse risk lower now? What supports are in place? What is the plan if cravings return?

A good taper plan includes follow-up visits, naloxone, support people, relapse-warning signs, and permission to pause or reverse the taper if risk increases.

Detox Is Not Enough

Detox is real medical treatment. It helps the body get through withdrawal more safely and more comfortably. But detox is not the whole treatment for addiction. Detox treats withdrawal — the deeper addiction treatment usually begins after detox, in residential rehab, outpatient treatment, medication treatment, therapy, recovery support, healthier routines, and rebuilding a life worth protecting.

⚠ Leaving detox without a medication plan can be dangerous Detoxification on its own, without medications for opioid use disorder, is not recommended because it increases the risk of returning to opioid use, overdose, and overdose death. Tolerance can drop quickly after detox, but cravings and relapse risk may remain high.

Common Concerns

"Is this just replacing one addiction with another?"

No. Addiction is not simply having a substance in the body. Addiction involves loss of control, compulsive use despite harm, craving, and life becoming organized around drug use.

When medication is taken as prescribed, the goal is stability: no intoxication, no withdrawal cycle, fewer cravings, lower overdose risk, and better ability to participate in life. That is treatment — not the same as active addiction.

"Will I feel high?"

The goal is not to feel high. The goal is to feel normal.

If a medication dose causes ongoing intoxication, confusion, oversedation, or unsafe behavior, the dose or treatment plan needs reassessment. If the dose is too low and cravings or withdrawal continue, that also needs reassessment.

"What if I still have cravings?"

Cravings are not a moral failure. Cravings are a symptom that needs a treatment response.

The response might include dose adjustment, more structure, counseling, coping strategies, addressing anxiety or sleep, changing people/places/routines, or switching medication if one option is not working. Tell your treatment team.

"What if I use while on medication?"

Tell your treatment team. Returning to use is clinically important information — not a reason to give up.

The safest response is usually to increase support, reassess dose adequacy, check for triggers, make an overdose prevention plan, and continue treatment engagement.

Take-Home Points

  • Methadone and buprenorphine both save lives. The biggest danger is often leaving treatment without ongoing medication support.
  • The best medication is the one that is effective, safe, tolerable, and realistic to continue after discharge.
  • Being out of treatment carries substantially higher overdose death risk — 4.8× higher for methadone, 3.3× higher for buprenorphine.
  • The first four weeks after stopping medication are especially dangerous.
  • Detox alone is not enough for most people with opioid use disorder. Recovery treatment continues through medication, rehab or outpatient care, support, routines, and time.
  • Have naloxone. Make sure the people around you know where it is and how to use it.
