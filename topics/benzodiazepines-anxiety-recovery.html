Benzodiazepines, Anxiety, Therapy, SSRIs, and Recovery from Substance Use Disorders

Why a medication that helps fast can still keep anxiety and addiction recovery stuck over time

This page is not about shaming you or saying your anxiety is not real.

It is about explaining why a medication that helps quickly can sometimes keep anxiety and addiction recovery stuck over time.

Many people say benzodiazepines are the only thing that really works for their anxiety. That feeling makes sense. But quick relief is not the same as long-term recovery.

Many people say benzodiazepines are the only thing that really works for their anxiety.

That experience is real. These medicines can reduce anxiety quickly. For many people, the relief can feel powerful and convincing.

But a medicine can help fast and still work poorly as a long-term plan. For many people with addiction histories, regular benzodiazepine use can lead to tolerance, rebound anxiety, dependence, and higher relapse risk over time.

Benzodiazepines are real anxiolytics with a strong short-term evidence base. The problem is not that they never help. The problem in severe substance use disorder populations is that their long-term risk-benefit balance is often unfavorable because of tolerance, physiologic dependence, misuse risk, cognitive effects, and overlap with other sedative pathways.

Benzodiazepines boost the brain’s main calming chemical (GABA). They create quick calm by turning brain activity down—not by teaching new coping skills.

Benzodiazepines work by boosting the effect of GABA, one of the brain’s main calming chemicals.

That means they can turn brain activity down quickly. They do not mainly help by teaching the brain new coping skills. They mainly help by chemically increasing inhibition.

That is one reason they can feel different from therapy or SSRIs. The effect is often fast, but it is mostly a chemical “turn-down” effect rather than a learning effect.

Benzodiazepines are positive allosteric modulators at the GABA-A receptor. They make the receptor respond more strongly to GABA, increasing inhibition in the central nervous system. That mechanism overlaps with some of alcohol’s calming effects—one reason benzodiazepines are used to treat alcohol withdrawal.

Alcohol can also reduce anxiety in the short term. Over time, both can make anxiety, sleep, and recovery worse.

Alcohol can also reduce anxiety for a short time. Many people have felt that before.

But over time, alcohol often makes anxiety, sleep, mood, and recovery worse. Benzodiazepines can create a similar long-term pattern for some people with addiction histories.

A substance can help fast and still keep the cycle going.

Alcohol and benzodiazepines both interact with inhibitory GABA-related systems, and cross-tolerance between them is well described in the medical literature. That overlap helps explain why benzodiazepines are standard treatment for alcohol withdrawal—but also why addiction psychiatrists are cautious about long-term benzodiazepine use in patients with alcohol use disorder and other substance use disorders.

Over time, the same dose may work less well. Anxiety between doses can feel like proof you need the medication—but sometimes it is partly rebound or withdrawal.

If a medicine keeps forcing anxiety down, the brain may adapt. Over time, the same dose may work less well. That is called tolerance.

Then a hard pattern can develop: anxiety shows up between doses, and it feels like proof that the medication is needed. But sometimes that anxiety is partly rebound or withdrawal, not just the original anxiety disorder getting worse.

This is not a character flaw. It is a brain adaptation that can happen with regular use.

Long-term benzodiazepine exposure is associated with neuroadaptation involving reduced inhibitory balance and increased excitability when the drug effect fades. This helps explain withdrawal, rebound symptoms, and interdose distress. More severe withdrawal can include tremor, agitation, perceptual symptoms, delirium, and seizures.

Opioids can reduce pain quickly, but with repeated use the nervous system can adapt. Benzodiazepines can follow a similar pattern with anxiety.

Think about opioids and pain. Opioids can reduce pain fast. But with repeated use, the nervous system can adapt, and pain sensitivity can sometimes increase over time.

Anxiety is not pain, and the biology is not identical. But the analogy is useful: quick symptom relief can sometimes teach the nervous system to depend on the drug instead of building stronger self-regulation.

Opioid-induced hyperalgesia and opioid tolerance are distinct but related phenomena involving neuroadaptation and increased symptom burden despite ongoing treatment. The benzodiazepine comparison is only an analogy. The teaching point is that repeated chemical suppression can reshape the system being treated.

Anxiety involves real brain circuits. Recovery is not just about shutting off the alarm—it is also about strengthening the systems that help you understand and manage it.

Anxiety is not “all in your head” in the dismissive sense. It involves real brain circuits.

The amygdala is like a fast alarm system. The insula helps you notice body signals. The hippocampus connects fear to memory and context. The prefrontal cortex helps you pause, evaluate, and choose what to do next.

Recovery from anxiety is not only about silencing the alarm. It is also about strengthening the brain systems that help you notice, interpret, tolerate, and respond differently.

Neurocircuit models of anxiety consistently implicate the amygdala, insula, hippocampus, and prefrontal regions in threat processing, interoception, contextual fear learning, and top-down regulation. Brain imaging studies suggest successful psychotherapy is often associated with altered amygdala and insula reactivity and stronger regulatory network function involving prefrontal circuitry.

Therapy helps the brain learn. Benzodiazepines lower anxiety chemically. Those are not the same kind of change—and some studies suggest they may partially conflict.

Psychotherapy helps the brain learn. Benzodiazepines lower anxiety chemically. Those are not the same kind of change.

In therapies like CBT and exposure-based treatment, the brain practices facing fear, noticing body signals, testing predictions, and learning that anxiety can rise and fall without escape or avoidance. That practice can build lasting coping.

Some studies suggest benzodiazepines may reduce part of the learning benefit from psychotherapy, especially exposure-based work. The combination may be less helpful than many people expect.

The clinical evidence on benzodiazepines and psychotherapy outcomes is mixed rather than absolute. Some human studies and reviews suggest benzodiazepines may weaken maintenance of gains or interfere with exposure-learning. Animal studies on fear-extinction more consistently raise concern that benzodiazepines can reduce extinction or make learning state-dependent.

Therapy is not just talking. It is practice. Repeated coping practice can change how anxiety-related brain circuits work.

Therapy is not “just talking.” Therapy is practice.

One trip to the gym does not build muscle. In the same way, one coping skill practice does not rewire anxiety. Repeated practice does.

Coping skills, exposure, mindfulness, urge-surfing, cognitive restructuring, distress tolerance, and staying present without using are like repetitions. They help train the brain’s recovery circuits.

Brain imaging studies suggest psychotherapy can change how anxiety-related circuits function. Practice can change the brain.

A growing neuroimaging literature supports the idea that psychotherapy is associated with measurable functional neural change in anxiety-related systems. The safest patient-facing phrasing is that psychotherapy “can change activity in anxiety-related circuits” rather than making strong claims about dramatic structural rewiring in every patient.

For moderate or severe anxiety, the best long-term treatment is often a combination of psychotherapy and a non-benzodiazepine medication when medication is needed.

For moderate or severe anxiety, the best long-term treatment is often a combination of psychotherapy and a non-benzodiazepine medication when medication is needed.

That matters even more in addiction recovery. When alcohol, pills, or other drugs are no longer available as coping tools, people usually need both safer medications and stronger behavior-based coping skills.

Clinical guidelines generally support SSRIs and SNRIs as first-line pharmacologic treatments for several anxiety disorders and support CBT and exposure-based psychotherapy as core evidence-based care. In substance use disorder populations, the clinical priority is to avoid reinforcing dependence-producing short-term coping strategies when safer longer-term treatments are available.

Physical dependence means the body has adapted to a medicine. Addiction involves craving, loss of control, and use despite harm. They are different—and both deserve care.

Physical dependence means the body has adapted to a medicine and can have withdrawal if it is stopped too fast. That is a biological response, not a moral failure.

Addiction involves a pattern like compulsive use, craving, loss of control, and continuing despite harm. A person can have dependence without addiction, and a person can have both.

This distinction is especially important in addiction psychiatry because patients may feel shamed when they develop expected physiologic dependence after regular prescribed use. Clear language reduces stigma and helps patients understand why tapering decisions require care without equating every case of dependence with benzodiazepine use disorder.

Many options can help. The goal is a plan that works over time without making addiction recovery more fragile.

Helpful options can include:

  • SSRIs or SNRIs
  • Buspirone for some people (especially generalized worry)
  • Hydroxyzine or propranolol for selected symptoms
  • CBT and exposure therapy
  • Mindfulness and distress tolerance
  • Better sleep habits and caffeine reduction
  • Exercise
  • Relapse-prevention work

No single option is right for everyone. The goal is not to pretend anxiety is easy. The goal is to build a plan that helps over time without making recovery more fragile.

SSRIs and SNRIs have the strongest broad anxiety-disorder evidence base among non-benzodiazepine medications. Buspirone is an option especially for generalized anxiety, though its effect may be more modest and it may work less well in people with prior benzodiazepine exposure. Mindfulness-based and CBT-informed approaches have evidence for anxiety reduction and some support in substance use disorder settings.

SSRIs don’t give quick relief—but that slow start is not failure. They work differently than benzodiazepines and don’t carry the same addiction risk.

SSRIs do not usually give an immediate “relief hit.” Instead, they gradually change serotonin signaling and downstream brain regulation over weeks.

That slower start does not mean they are failing. For many anxiety disorders, some improvement may appear around 4–6 weeks. Fuller benefit often takes 6–8 weeks or longer after a useful dose is reached.

They also do not create the same intoxication, reinforcement, and tolerance cycle that makes benzodiazepines risky for many people with substance use disorders.

A full trial means enough dose plus enough time. Failure of one SSRI does not mean all SSRIs or all non-benzodiazepine anxiety treatments will fail.

⚠️ If you are already taking a benzodiazepine

Do not stop it suddenly on your own. Withdrawal can be dangerous and can include seizures.

If a benzodiazepine needs to be reduced, the plan should be individualized with a clinician. This page does not provide a taper schedule.

🚨 Safety

This is general education, not individualized medical advice.

Get urgent help right away for:

  • Suicidal thoughts
  • Seizure
  • Severe confusion
  • Severe withdrawal symptoms
  • Chest pain or trouble breathing
  • Any medical emergency

Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room.
